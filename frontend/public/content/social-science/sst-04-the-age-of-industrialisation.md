## 3. SYLLABUS-ALIGNED COMPLETE REVISION NOTES

### Section 1: Before the Industrial Revolution (Proto-Industrialisation)

#### 1. The Concept of Proto-Industrialisation
* **Definition**: Proto-industrialisation refers to the phase of large-scale industrial production for an international market that took place **prior to the establishment of factories**.
* **Key Features**:
  * Production was **not factory-based**; it was organized within decentralized rural households in the countryside.
  * Controlled by town-based merchants who supplied capital and raw materials to rural peasants and artisans.
  * Served as an early network of commercial exchanges without steam-driven machinery.

#### 2. The Merchant-Peasant Relationship in the Countryside
* **Shift to Villages**: In 17th and 18th century Europe, urban trade guilds held legal monopolies over production and trade in towns. They regulated prices, restricted competition, and limited entry of new merchants.
* **Rural Production Network**:
  * Merchants moved to the countryside, offering advance payments to poor peasants and cottagers.
  * Rural families, struggling with shrinking landholdings due to open-field enclosures, welcomed merchant advances to supplement agricultural income.
  * Entire households participated: family members worked at different stages—carding, spinning, weaving, and dyeing.
* **Production Chain**:
  * A merchant clothier bought wool from a **stapler** (one who sorts wool according to fiber).
  * Wool was carried to **spinners**, then to **weavers**, **fullers** (who gathered/pleated cloth), and **dyers**.
  * The finishing was done in **London** before exporting. Hence, London became known as a **finishing centre**.
  * Each merchant employed about 20 to 25 workers at each stage of production.

#### 3. Coming Up of the Factory
* **First Factories**: Emerged in England in the **1730s**, but multiplied rapidly in the late 18th century.
* **Cotton Industry**: Became the symbol of the new era. Cotton imports into Britain soared from 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to 22 million pounds by 1787.
* **Richard Arkwright's Cotton Mill**:
  * Arkwright created the **cotton mill**, bringing machinery, workers, and raw materials under one roof.
  * Allowed careful supervision of the production process, quality control, and regulation of labour that was impossible in the scattered countryside.

#### 4. The Pace of Industrial Change
* **Dominant Sectors**: Cotton and Iron/Steel were the leading industrial sectors.
  * Cotton led industrialization up to the 1840s.
  * With railway expansion in Britain (1840s) and colonies (1860s), the **iron and steel industry** surpassed cotton. By 1873, Britain exported iron and steel worth £77 million (double its cotton exports).
* **Persistence of Traditional Sectors**: New mechanized factories did not immediately displace traditional industries. Less than 20% of the total workforce was employed in technologically advanced industrial sectors by the mid-19th century.
* **Slow Adoption of Steam Power**:
  * High cost of steam engines and technological caution among industrialists.
  * **James Watt** improved Thomas Newcomen's steam engine and patented it in **1781**. His friend **Matthew Boulton** manufactured the new model.
  * Industrialists were slow to adopt it; at the beginning of the 19th century, there were only 321 steam engines across Britain.

### Section 2: Hand Labour and Steam Power

#### 1. Abundance of Labour and Industrial Preference
* **Labour Surplus**: In 19th-century Britain, there was no shortage of human labour. Poor peasants migrated to cities in large numbers in search of jobs, keeping wages low.
* **Preference for Hand Labour**:
  * **Seasonal Demand**: Many industries required labour only during specific seasons (e.g., gasworks and breweries in winter; bookbinding and printing before Christmas; ship repair in spring). Industrialists preferred hiring seasonal hand labour over installing expensive machinery.
  * **Intricate Designs & Custom Goods**: Machines produced uniform, standardized goods for mass markets. However, upper classes (aristocrats and bourgeoisie) preferred handmade goods symbolizing refinement, class, and custom craftsmanship.

#### 2. Life of the Workers
* **Job Competition & Social Networks**:
  * Landing a job depended heavily on personal networks and friendship ties with existing factory workers.
  * Job-seekers without connections spent weeks waiting, sleeping under bridges, in night shelters, or in casual wards set up by Poor Law authorities.
* **Seasonality of Work**: Prolonged periods of unemployment after seasonal peaks forced workers back onto the streets or into temporary casual labour.
* **Real Wages vs. Inflation**: Though nominal wages increased in the early 19th century, high prices during Napoleonic wars eroded real income.
* **Hostility to New Technology (The Spinning Jenny)**:
  * Invention of the **Spinning Jenny** by **James Hargreaves** in **1764** speeded up the spinning process (one wheel set in motion multiple spindles).
  * Female spinners, fearing unemployment, attacked Spinning Jennies across English towns.
* **Infrastructure Boom (1840s onwards)**: Building of railways, tunnels, roads, drainage, and sewers after the 1840s created massive employment opportunities for urban labourers.

### Section 3: Industrialisation in the Colonies (India)

#### 1. The Age of Indian Textiles (Pre-Industrial Period)
* **Global Supremacy**: Prior to machine industrialization, Indian silk and cotton goods dominated the international textile market. Indian fine-quality cotton was unmatched in global markets.
* **Pre-Colonial Trade Networks**:
  * Vibrant sea trade operated through ancient port cities:
    * **Surat** (Gujarat coast) connected India to the Gulf and Red Sea ports.
    * **Hooghly** (Bengal) and **Masulipatnam** (Coromandel coast) traded with Southeast Asian ports.
  * Managed by Indian merchants, financiers, and supply networks who advanced credit to weavers and transported woven cloth to ports.

#### 2. Colonial Interruption & Decline of Pre-Colonial Ports
* **European Monopoly & Concessions**: By the 1750s, European companies (East India Company, French, Dutch) secured trade concessions from local royal courts and monopoly rights to trade.
* **Shift to Colonial Ports**:
  * Pre-colonial ports of **Surat** and **Hooghly** decayed as trade through them collapsed. Exports from Surat plummeted from ₹16 million in the late 17th century to ₹3 million by the 1740s.
  * Colonial port cities of **Bombay** and **Calcutta** grew rapidly under European control, marking the shift from traditional Indian merchant networks to colonial trade patterns.

#### 3. The Condition of Weavers and the Gomasthas
* **East India Company's Monopoly**: After securing political power in Bengal and Carnatic in the 1760s, the British East India Company established direct control over textile production to eliminate competition from French, Dutch, and Portuguese traders.
* **Appointment of Gomasthas**:
  * The Company appointed paid servants called **Gomasthas** to supervise weavers, collect supplies, and inspect cloth quality.
  * **System of Advances**: Weavers were given loans to purchase raw materials. Once an advance was taken, weavers were contractually bound to deliver the finished cloth exclusively to the Company.
* **Clash Between Weavers and Gomasthas**:
  * Gomasthas were outsiders with no social link to villages. They acted arrogantly, marched into villages with sepoys and peons, and punished weavers with beatings for delays.
  * Weavers lost the freedom to bargain or sell to other buyers at higher market rates.
  * Consequence: Many weavers deserted villages, migrated to other districts, or abandoned weaving to become agricultural labourers.

#### 4. Manchester Comes to India
* **Decline of Indian Textile Exports**:
  * British textile manufacturers pressured the British government to impose heavy import duties on Indian cotton textiles entering Britain.
  * Simultaneously, Manchester machine-made cotton goods were flooded into the Indian market tariff-free.
* **Dual Plight of Indian Weavers (1850s–1860s)**:
  1. **Collapse of Export Market**: Indian weaver exports drastically declined. By 1850–51, piece-goods exports fell below 3%.
  2. **Invasion of Domestic Market**: Cheap, durable Manchester machine-goods captured Indian retail markets.
  3. **Raw Cotton Crisis (1860s)**: During the **American Civil War** (1861–1865), American raw cotton supplies to Britain were cut off. Britain bought massive quantities of raw cotton from India, causing raw cotton prices in India to skyrocket. Indian weavers could no longer afford raw material.

### Section 4: Factories Come Up (Early Entrepreneurs & Workers)

#### 1. Early Indian Capitalists & Capital Accumulation
Many Indian entrepreneurs made their initial fortunes through the **China trade** (exporting Indian opium to China and importing tea to Britain) or through domestic trade networks.

| Entrepreneur | Region / Base | Major Industrial Contributions & Achievements |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Dwarkanath Tagore** | Bengal | Made fortune in China trade; set up 6 joint-stock companies in Bengal in the 1830s–1840s. |
| **Dinshaw Petit** | Bombay | Parsi industrialist; built early cotton mills in Bombay. |
| **Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata** | Bombay & Jamshedpur | Parsi capitalist; built cotton mills and founded the **Tata Iron and Steel Company (TISCO)** at Jamshedpur (1907). |
| **Seth Hukumchand** | Calcutta | Marwari businessman; established the **first Indian-owned Jute Mill** in Calcutta in **1917**. |
| **G.D. Birla** | Calcutta & National | Prominent Marwari industrialist; built a diversified manufacturing empire from trade profits. |

#### 2. Colonial Restrictions on Indian Capital
* European Managing Agencies (such as **Bird Heiglers & Co.**, **Andrew Yule**, and **Jardine Skinner & Co.**) controlled vast proportions of Indian industrial capital.
* Indian businessmen were barred from joining European chambers of commerce and restricted to trade in raw materials (raw cotton, opium, jute, food grains) needed by Britain.

#### 3. Recruitment of Workers: The Jobber System
* **Demand for Industrial Labour**: As cotton, jute, and steel mills expanded, thousands of peasants and landless agricultural workers migrated from surrounding rural districts (e.g., Ratnagiri to Bombay mills) in search of work.
* **Role of the Jobber**:
  * Industrialists hired an old and trusted worker called a **Jobber** to recruit new workers.
  * The Jobber enjoyed immense authority: he got people from his village, provided them jobs, helped them settle in cities, and advanced them money during crises.
  * Over time, Jobbers began demanding money, gifts, and favors for granting jobs, controlling the lives of workers.

### Section 5: The Peculiarities of Industrial Growth

#### 1. Focus on Specific Non-Competitive Products
* European industrial houses in India focused on export-oriented products non-competitive with British home industries: tea, coffee, indigo, jute, and mining.
* Early Indian textile mills produced **coarse cotton yarn** rather than finished cloth. Yarn was exported to China or used by Indian handloom weavers.

#### 2. Impact of the Swadeshi Movement (1905)
* The **Swadeshi Movement** mobilized people to boycott foreign cloth and buy Indian-made goods.
* Indian industrial groups organized themselves to demand tariff protection and concessions.
* Textile industrialists shifted from yarn production to **woven piece-goods** (cloth) as yarn exports to China declined due to Chinese and Japanese competition.

#### 3. First World War: A Major Turning Point for Indian Industry
* **British Industrial Diversion**: During WWI (1914–1918), British mills were diverted to produce war materials. Imports of Manchester goods into India declined sharply.
* **Indian Market Expansion**: Indian factories were called upon to supply war requirements: jute bags, cloth for army uniforms, tents, boots, and wooden cart wheels.
* **Long-Term Consequence**:
  * New factories were set up and existing units ran multiple shifts.
  * Post-WWI, Manchester could never recapture its dominant position in the Indian market. The British economy collapsed under war debt and competition from the USA, Japan, and Germany.

#### 4. Predominance of Small-Scale Industries
* Despite factory growth, large-scale mechanized industries formed only a small segment of the economy. In 1911, only 5% of total industrial workers lived in factory centers like Bombay and Calcutta (10% by 1931).
* **Handloom Expansion & Fly Shuttle**:
  * Handloom cloth production expanded steadily in the 20th century.
  * Weavers adopted technology like the **fly shuttle** (a mechanical device operated by ropes and pulleys that speeds up weaving and handles wider looms).
  * By 1931, over 35% of handlooms in India were fitted with fly shuttles (up to 70–80% in Travancore, Cochin, Mysore, Bengal, and Madras).

### Section 6: Market for Goods (Advertising Strategies)

Industrialists used persuasive marketing tools to create consumer desire and establish brand loyalty in India.

#### 1. Product Labels ("Made in Manchester")
* Foreign manufacturers attached prominent labels to cloth bundles.
* The label carried the place of manufacture ("Made in Manchester") in bold text to signify quality assurance.
* **Use of Gods and Goddesses**: Labels featured striking illustrations of Indian gods and goddesses (e.g., **Krishna**, **Saraswati**, **Lakshmi**). This gave foreign goods a familiar, sacred, and culturally acceptable appearance to Indian buyers.

#### 2. Printing Calendars
* Calendars were distributed widely to tea shops, offices, middle-class homes, and poor households.
* Unlike newspapers or posters, calendars hung on walls for an entire year, ensuring continuous visual repetition of brand logos and products.

#### 3. Figures of Royalty and Nawabs
* Advertisements featured portraits of historical emperors, maharajas, and nawabs.
* Message: *"If this product is endorsed or used by royalty, its quality is unquestionable."*

#### 4. Nationalist Appeal
* Indian manufacturers used advertisements to spread swadeshi and patriotic messages.
* Slogan/Theme: *"If you care about the nation, buy products made in India."*

## 5. COMPARATIVE STRUCTURAL MATRICES

### A. Proto-Industrialisation vs. Factory-Based Industrialisation

| Comparison Parameter | Proto-Industrial System | Factory-Based Industrial System |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Location** | Countryside / Rural Households | Urban Industrial Cities & Centralized Factories |
| **Production Machinery** | Traditional hand tools (hand-spindles, looms) | Powered machinery (Steam engines, Powerlooms) |
| **Capital & Supervision** | Merchant clothier advancing cash; decentralized | Factory owner managing under one roof |
| **Control of Work** | Artisans worked at their own pace in family units | Strict factory discipline, fixed hours, wage labour |
| **Output Scale** | Small, scattered batches per household | Mass, standardized production at high speed |

### B. Pre-Colonial Indian Ports vs. Colonial Port Cities

| Structural Feature | Pre-Colonial Ports (Surat & Hooghly) | Colonial Port Cities (Bombay & Calcutta) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Era of Dominance** | Up to the mid-18th century (Pre-1750s) | Late 18th century onwards (Post-1750s) |
| **Controlling Network** | Indian merchants, bankers, and local rulers | European chartered trading companies |
| **Primary Trade Routes** | Gulf, Red Sea, and Southeast Asian ports | Export routes to Europe and China |
| **Economic Fate** | Collapsed under European monopoly trade | Flourished into major industrial and administrative hubs |

## 6. VERIFIED PREVIOUS-YEAR BOARD QUESTIONS (PYQs)

### Question 1 (BSER Main Exam 2024, Paper S-08, Q5)
**Question**: What is Industrialization? *(औद्योगीकरण किसे कहते हैं?)* [02 Marks]  
**Model Answer**:  
Industrialization is the economic and social process characterized by the transition from an agrarian economy based on manual hand labour to an industrial economy dominated by machine-based manufacturing, factory production, division of labour, and technological innovation.

### Question 2 (BSER Main Exam 2023, Paper S-08, Q14)
**Question**: How did the lives of workers get affected due to abundance of labour in the market? Write. [03 Marks]  
**OR**  
What impact did the inventions of the eighteenth century have on the production process? Describe it. [03 Marks]  
**Model Answer (Option 1)**:  
The abundance of labour in 19th-century Britain affected workers' lives in the following ways:
1. **Low Wages & Job Uncertainty**: A vast pool of rural migrants reduced wage rates. Finding a job depended heavily on personal connections and networks with existing factory workers.
2. **Prolonged Periods of Unemployment**: Most urban industries were seasonal (e.g., breweries, printing, gasworks). After seasonal work ended, workers were forced back onto the streets, sleeping in night shelters or casual wards.
3. **Hostility to Machinery**: Fearing loss of employment, workers fiercely resisted new technology. For instance, female spinners attacked the Spinning Jenny introduced by James Hargreaves in 1764.

### Question 3 (BSER Board Supplementary Exam 2024, Paper S-124, Q12)
**Question**: Who were the Gomasthas? Why did clashes occur between weavers and Gomasthas? [03 Marks]  
**Model Answer**:  
1. **Who were Gomasthas**: Gomasthas were paid servants appointed by the British East India Company to supervise weavers, collect finished textile supplies, and inspect cloth quality.
2. **Causes of Clashes**:
   * **Arrogant Behavior**: Unlike traditional Indian traders who had social ties with villagers, Gomasthas were outsiders who entered villages accompanied by sepoys and peons, beating weavers for delays.
   * **Loss of Bargaining Power**: Under the system of advances, weavers were contractually bound to deliver cloth exclusively to the Company at low prices, losing the freedom to sell to other buyers.

### Question 4 (BSER Official Model Paper 2026–27, Q4)
**Question**: Explain how the First World War proved to be a boon for Indian industries. [03 Marks]  
**Model Answer**:  
The First World War (1914–1918) transformed Indian industry due to the following factors:
1. **Decline in British Imports**: British factories were repurposed to meet wartime military demands. Imports of Manchester cloth into India declined drastically, leaving the home market open to Indian mills.
2. **Wartime Supply Orders**: Indian factories were contracted by the colonial government to supply war materials such as jute sandbags, army uniform cloth, boots, tents, and leather goods.
3. **Establishment of New Units**: To meet rising demand, new factories were set up, old units worked multiple shifts, and thousands of new workers were employed. This permanently strengthened India's domestic industrial base.

## 7. SHORT & LONG ANSWER PRACTICE BANK

### Short Answer Questions (2–3 Marks)

#### Q1. What is meant by "Proto-industrialisation"? Why did merchants move to the countryside in 17th-century Europe?
**Answer**:  
Proto-industrialisation refers to the early phase of industrial development prior to the setup of factories, where production occurred in decentralized rural homes for international markets. Merchants moved to the countryside because urban trade guilds in towns held legal monopolies over trade, regulated prices, and restricted new entrants. In contrast, rural peasants welcomed merchant advances to supplement their shrinking agricultural earnings.

#### Q2. Explain the functioning of the "Jobber" system in recruitment of industrial workers in India.
**Answer**:  
1. A **Jobber** was an old and trusted worker employed by factory owners to recruit new workers.
2. He brought workers from his native village, promised them jobs, helped them settle in cities, and provided financial assistance during emergencies.
3. Over time, Jobbers acquired immense power and began demanding money, gifts, and favors in exchange for employment opportunities.

#### Q3. Describe the role played by advertisements and product labels in creating a market for goods in 19th-century India.
**Answer**:  
1. **Quality Assurance**: Labels marked "Made in Manchester" assured buyers of manufacturing quality.
2. **Cultural Familiarity**: Labels featured images of Indian gods and goddesses (Krishna, Saraswati) to make foreign cloth look familiar and divinely sanctioned.
3. **Endorsement by Royalty**: Images of Maharajas and Nawabs conveyed that using the product was a mark of prestige and royal approval.

---

### Long Answer / Essay Questions (4 Marks)

#### Q4. "The Swadeshi Movement and the First World War gave a major impetus to Indian industrial growth." Evaluate this statement with historical evidence.
**Answer**:  
The development of Indian industries received two major boosts in the early 20th century:

**I. Impact of the Swadeshi Movement (1905)**:
1. **Boycott of Foreign Goods**: The Swadeshi Movement rallied citizens to burn foreign cloth and buy Indian-made products, spurring demand for indigenous textiles.
2. **Shift in Production**: As Chinese market demand for Indian yarn declined due to Japanese competition, Indian cotton industrialists shifted from spinning yarn to weaving piece-goods (cloth).
3. **Collective Industrial Demands**: Indian entrepreneurs organized trade associations to press the colonial government for tariff protection and tax relief.

**II. Impact of the First World War (1914–1918)**:
1. **Reduction in British Competition**: British industrial capacity was directed entirely toward manufacturing war goods, causing a steep decline in British exports to India.
2. **Colonial Procurement Orders**: Indian mills were engaged to produce war supplies including jute bags, uniform cloth, tents, boots, and cart wheels.
3. **Long-Term Industrial Gains**: Existing factories worked multiple shifts and new factories were established. Post-war, British manufacturers could never reclaim their lost dominance in the Indian market, solidifying the position of Indian home industries.

    (B) To make foreign products look familiar and culturally acceptable  
    (C) To pay respect to Indian culture  
    (D) Because British artists could only draw divine figures  
    * **Answer**: (B)  
    * **Explanation**: Images of gods (Krishna, Saraswati) gave foreign goods a familiar, divine appearance to boost local sales.

14. **Which joint-stock pioneer set up six companies in Bengal during the 1830s–1840s?**  
    (A) G.D. Birla  
    (B) Dwarkanath Tagore  
    (C) Jamsetjee Tata  
    (D) Dadabhai Naoroji  
    * **Answer**: (B)  
    * **Explanation**: Dwarkanath Tagore built six joint-stock companies in Bengal using China trade capital.

15. **What percentage of total industrial workers lived in major factory hubs like Bombay and Calcutta in 1911?**  
    (A) 5%  
    (B) 25%  
    (C) 50%  
    (D) 75%  
    * **Answer**: (A)  
    * **Explanation**: In 1911, only 5% of total industrial workers were in major factory centers (10% by 1931), showing that small-scale units predominated.
