## SECTION 2: Complete Syllabus-Aligned Revision Notes

### Theme 1: Water Scarcity and the Need for Water Conservation
#### 1. Global and National Water Availability
* **Global Distribution**: Approximately **97.5%** of the earth's water is saline (oceans and seas). Only **2.5%** exists as fresh water. Of this 2.5% freshwater, nearly **70%** occurs as ice sheets and glaciers in Antarctica, Greenland, and mountainous regions, leaving less than **30%** stored as groundwater and surface runoff.
* **India's Share**: India receives about **4%** of global precipitation and ranks **133rd** in the world in terms of water availability per person per annum.
* **Total Renewable Water Resources**: India's total renewable water resources are estimated at **1,897 sq km per annum**. By 2025, large parts of India are predicted to join countries or regions facing absolute water scarcity.

#### 2. Quantitative vs. Qualitative Water Scarcity
Water scarcity is the shortage of water relative to demand. It is driven by two distinct dimensions:
* **Quantitative Scarcity (Over-exploitation and Unequal Access)**:
  * **Population Explosion**: Rapid population growth requires more water for domestic consumption and food grain production.
  * **Expansion of Irrigated Agriculture**: Intensive farming practices (e.g., tube wells, high-yielding crop varieties) lead to excessive groundwater extraction, causing a drastic fall in subterranean water tables.
  * **Industrialisation and Urbanisation**: Post-independence industrial growth and expanding urban centers with modern lifestyles increase energy and water demands. Hydroelectric power contributes approximately **22%** of India's total electricity generation.
  * **Seasonal and Spatial Variation**: Scarcity is often associated with arid or semi-arid regions with low or highly erratic rainfall (e.g., Western Rajasthan).
* **Qualitative Scarcity (Pollution and Contamination)**:
  * Even in areas with abundant rainfall and surface water, water can become unusable due to degradation.
  * Main pollutants include domestic sewage, untreated industrial effluents, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and heavy metal runoff from intensive agriculture and manufacturing.

### Theme 2: Multi-Purpose River Projects and Integrated Water Resources Management
#### 1. Concept and Historical Antecedents of Dams
* **Definition of a Dam**: A dam is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs, or retards the flow, creating a reservoir, lake, or impoundment. "Dam" refers to the reservoir rather than just the physical structure.
* **Ancient Hydraulic Structures in India**:
  * **1st Century BCE**: *Sringaverapura* near Allahabad built a sophisticated water harvesting system harvesting the floodwaters of the Ganga river.
  * **Mauryan Era (3rd Century BCE)**: Extensive construction of dams, lakes, and irrigation systems during Chandragupta Maurya's reign (e.g., *Sudarshan Lake* in Junagadh, Gujarat).
  * **11th Century CE**: *Bhopal Lake*, one of the largest artificial lakes of its time, was constructed.
  * **14th Century CE**: *Iltutmish* constructed the tank at *Hauz Khas* in Delhi for supplying water to the Siri Fort area.

#### 2. Functions and Benefits of Multi-Purpose Projects
Modern dams are termed **Multi-Purpose River Projects** because they integrate several socio-economic objectives simultaneously:
1. **Irrigation**: Channeling reservoir water to agricultural fields via canal networks.
2. **Hydroelectric Power Generation**: Harnessing kinetic energy of flowing water for clean energy.
3. **Domestic and Industrial Water Supply**: Piped municipal water supply for towns and industries.
4. **Flood Control**: Regulating river discharges during monsoon seasons (e.g., Hirakud Dam on Mahanadi).
5. **Inland Navigation**: Waterways created by reservoirs and deep canals.
6. **Fish Breeding and Pisciculture**: Reservoir fisheries supporting commercial fish farming.
7. **Recreation and Tourism**: Boating and ecological parks developed around reservoir catchments.

> **Key Historical Quote**: Jawaharlal Nehru proudly proclaimed multi-purpose dams as the **"Temples of Modern India"**, believing they would integrate agricultural development and rural economy with rapid industrialisation and urban growth.

### Theme 3: Environmental and Social Criticisms of Large Dams

While multi-purpose projects offer substantial benefits, they have faced intense criticism and public opposition from environmentalists, social activists, and displaced communities.

| Critical Parameter | Ecological / Social Impact |
| :--- | :--- |
| **River Regime Disturbance** | Damming and regulating natural flows leads to excessive sedimentation at the reservoir bottom, turning riverbeds into rocky substrates and destroying aquatic fauna habitats. |
| **Fragmentation of Rivers** | Physical barriers impede the natural migration of aquatic animals, especially migratory fish species, during breeding seasons. |
| **Submergence of Forests & Soils** | Reservoirs submerge vast tracts of existing forests, arable land, and native vegetation, causing organic decomposition and greenhouse gas release (methane). |
| **Involuntary Displacement** | Large dams cause displacement of local indigenous and peasant communities, depriving them of land, livelihood, and ancestral heritage without adequate compensation or rehabilitation. |
| **Inter-State Water Disputes** | Conflict over sharing water volume, costs, and benefits (e.g., *Cauvery Water Dispute* between Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, *Krishna-Godavari Dispute* over Andhra Pradesh/Telangana/Maharashtra). |
| **Induced Seismicity & Siltation** | Heavy water pressure in large reservoirs can trigger earthquakes. Submerged silt fails to reach downstream floodplains, accelerating soil erosion and land degradation. |
| **Failure in Flood Control** | Paradoxically, releasing excess reservoir water during extreme rainfall events has triggered catastrophic floods in downstream regions (e.g., Maharashtra and Gujarat floods in 2006). |

#### Major Anti-Dam Social Movements
1. **Narmada Bachao Andolan (Save Narmada Movement)**: Led by Medha Patkar against the construction of the *Sardar Sarovar Dam* on the Narmada River in Gujarat, focusing on environmental degradation and rehabilitation of tribal refugees.
2. **Tehri Dam Andolan**: Anti-dam protest against the high-rockfill *Tehri Dam* on the Bhagirathi River in Uttarakhand due to seismic vulnerability in the Himalayas and community displacement.

### Theme 4: Rainwater Harvesting Systems (Traditional and Modern)

Rainwater Harvesting (RWH) is an eco-friendly, low-cost, and socio-culturally accepted alternative to large multi-purpose projects.

#### 1. Traditional Rainwater Harvesting Techniques in India

##### A. Hill and Mountain Regions
* **Guls and Kuls**: Diversion channels constructed in Western Himalayan regions (e.g., Himachal Pradesh and Jammu & Kashmir) to channel water from glacier-fed streams directly into agricultural terraces.

##### B. Arid and Semi-Arid Regions of Rajasthan
* **Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting**: Capturing rain falling on house roofs via PVC/earthen pipes and directing it to underground storage tanks called *Tankas*.
* **Khading and Johads**:
  * **Khadins**: Agricultural fields converted into rain-fed storage structures in Jaisalmer. Embankments (*Khadin dykes*) across sloping ground capture surface runoff, allowing water to saturate the soil for winter (*Rabi*) crops.
  * **Johads**: Small earthen check-dams built across natural streams in other parts of Rajasthan (e.g., Alwar) to store surface water and recharge groundwater tables.
* **Stepwells (Baolis)**: Multi-storeyed traditional community wells designed to collect rainwater runoff and supply clean drinking water during dry spells.

##### C. Floodplains of Bengal
* **Inundation Canals**: Farmers in Bengal constructed shallow canals to channel floodwaters from rivers into agricultural fields, providing irrigation and nutrient-rich silt.

##### D. North-Eastern Hill Regions (Meghalaya)
* **Bamboo Drip Irrigation System**: A 200-year-old traditional system in Meghalaya where bamboo pipes are used to divert perennial spring water over long distances to water black pepper and betel nut plantations, delivering 20 to 80 drops per minute directly at the plant root.

---

#### 2. Case Study: Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting via 'Tankas' in Arid Rajasthan

In the arid and semi-arid regions of Rajasthan—particularly in **Bikaner, Phalodi, and Barmer**—almost all traditional houses constructed underground tanks called **Tankas** for storing drinking water.

```
       [ Rainwater Falls on Slanted/Flat Rooftop ]
                           │
                           ▼
              [ PVC / Conduit Pipe Net ]
                           │
                           ▼
          [ First Spell Water Discarded (Cleaning) ]
                           │
                           ▼
       [ Clean Water Flows into Underground Tanka ]
                           │
                           ▼
[ Stored Water Remains Cool & Potable Year-Round ('Palar Pani') ]
```

##### Structural and Operational Features of a 'Tanka':
1. **Size and Location**: Constructed inside the main house or courtyard (*Aangan*). A typical Tanka in Phalodi measures 6.1 metres deep, 4.27 metres long, and 2.44 metres wide.
2. **Connectivity**: Connected to the sloping roofs of houses through a network of pipes.
3. **First Spell Cleaning**: The first spell of rain is discarded to clean the rooftop and pipes; subsequent rains are directed into the Tanka.
4. **Palar Pani**: Rainwater stored in sealed underground Tankas is called ***Palar Pani*** in local parlance. It is considered the purest form of natural drinking water in Rajasthan.
5. **Micro-Climate Regulation**: Rooms constructed around underground Tankas stay significantly cooler during hot summer months due to thermal insulation.
6. **Decline and Modern Shift**: Traditional Tanka usage has declined in parts of Rajasthan due to the availability of year-round water from the **Indira Gandhi Canal** (*Rajasthan Canal*). However, many households still prefer Palar Pani over canal water due to taste and quality.

---

#### 3. Modern Success Stories in Rainwater Harvesting

##### A. Gendathur Village Case Study (Mysuru, Karnataka)
* **Location**: Gendathur, a remote village in Mysuru district, Karnataka.
* **Achievement**: Households have installed rooftop rainwater harvesting systems on nearly **200 houses**, earning the village the distinction of being rich in rainwater harvesting.
* **Water Yield**: The village receives an average annual rainfall of 1,000 mm. With an 80% collection efficiency, each house collects nearly **50,000 litres** of water annually. Collectively, 200 houses harvest **1,00,000 litres (100 cu m)** of rainwater every year.

##### B. Statutory Mandatory Harvesting in Tamil Nadu
* **State Policy**: **Tamil Nadu** became the first state in India to make rooftop rainwater harvesting **compulsory** for all houses across the state.
* **Legal Enforcement**: Defaulting homeowners face legal penalties, leading to widespread adoption and groundwater recovery.

### 3.1 Historical Development Timeline of Water Management
* **1st Century BCE**: Construction of sophisticated floodwater harvesting systems at *Sringaverapura* near Allahabad (Ganga River).
* **3rd Century BCE**: Extensive dam and canal network construction under Chandragupta Maurya.
* **11th Century CE**: Construction of the artificial *Bhopal Lake*.
* **14th Century CE**: Construction of the *Hauz Khas* reservoir in Delhi by Sultan Iltutmish.
* **1948–1953**: Inauguration of independent India's major multi-purpose projects (*Damodar Valley Corporation*, *Bhakra Nangal Project*).
* **1973–1980s**: Rise of anti-dam social movements (*Narmada Bachao Andolan*, *Tehri Dam Andolan*).
* **2001**: Tamil Nadu makes rooftop rainwater harvesting legally mandatory for all buildings.

### 3.3 Comparative Matrix: Multi-Purpose Projects vs. Traditional Rainwater Harvesting

| Parameter | Multi-Purpose River Projects (Dams) | Traditional Rainwater Harvesting (RWH) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Scale & Investment** | Large-scale, high capital investment, long gestation period. | Decentralised, low-cost, community-managed or individual level. |
| **Ecological Impact** | High risk of deforestation, river fragmentation, and habitat destruction. | Eco-friendly with zero deforestation or ecological disturbance. |
| **Social Cost** | Causes large-scale involuntary displacement of indigenous populations. | Zero displacement; empowers local communities directly. |
| **Water Quality** | Prone to industrial/agricultural runoff contamination in storage reservoirs. | Cleanest potable water (*Palar Pani*), minimal treatment required. |
| **Primary Advantage** | Generates bulk hydroelectric power and multi-district irrigation. | Sustains drinking water needs in dry seasons and recharges groundwater. |

#### Question 1
**Differentiate between Khadins and Johads used for rainwater harvesting in Rajasthan.**

**Answer**:
