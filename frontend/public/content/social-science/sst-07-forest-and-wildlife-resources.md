## Syllabus-Aligned Complete Revision Notes

### Theme 1: Biodiversity and Flora/Fauna in India

#### 1. What is Biodiversity?
- **Definition**: **Biodiversity** (or Biological Diversity) refers to the immensely enriched variety of life forms—including plants, animals, and microorganisms—existing in a specific habitat or on Earth, interlinked in an intricate web of ecological systems.
- **Ecological Importance**: Humans, along with all living organisms, form a complex web of ecological balance. For instance, plants, animals, and microorganisms re-create the air we breathe, the water we drink, and the soil that produces our food.

#### 2. India's Biodiversity Status
- India is one of the world's richest countries in terms of vast biological diversity.
- India possesses nearly **8% of the total number of species in the world** (estimated to be 1.6 million species).
- Over **81,000 species of fauna** (animals) and **47,000 species of flora** (plants) are documented in India.
- About **15,000 flowering plant species** are endemic (native) to India.

#### 3. IUCN Classification of Species (International Union for Conservation of Nature)
Based on existence and risk of extinction, plants and species are classified into six categories:

| IUCN Category | Definition / Characteristic | Prescribed Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Normal Species** | Population levels are considered normal for their survival. | Cattle, Sal, Pine, Rodents. |
| **Endangered Species** | Species in danger of extinction; survival is difficult if negative factors continue. | Blackbuck, Crocodile, Indian wild ass, Indian rhino, Lion-tailed macaque, Sangai (brow-antered deer in Manipur). |
| **Vulnerable Species** | Population has declined to levels where it is likely to move into endangered category in near future. | Blue sheep, Asiatic elephant, Gangetic dolphin. |
| **Rare Species** | Species with small population that may move into endangered/vulnerable if negative factors affect them. | Himalayan brown bear, Wild Asiatic buffalo, Desert fox, Hornbill. |
| **Endemic Species** | Species found only in particular specific areas isolated by natural or geographical barriers. | Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig, Mithun in Arunachal Pradesh. |
| **Extinct Species** | Species not found after search in known or likely areas where they occurred. | Asiatic cheetah (*Acinonyx jubatus* - declared extinct in India in **1952**), Pink-headed duck. |

### Theme 2: Causes of Depletion of Flora and Fauna

#### 1. Key Drivers of Forest and Wildlife Depletion
1. **Colonial Exploitation**: Expansion of railways, agriculture, commercial timber plantations, mining, and scientific forestry during the British colonial period.
2. **Agricultural Expansion**: According to the Forest Survey of India, between 1951 and 1980, over **26,200 sq. km of forest area** was converted into agricultural land across India.
3. **River Valley Projects & Infrastructure**: Large-scale development projects flooded vast forest tracts:
   - **Narmada Sagar Project** in Madhya Pradesh inundated over 40,000 hectares of forest.
4. **Mining Activities**: Mining leads to severe deforestation and habitat fragmentation:
   - **Buxa Tiger Reserve** in West Bengal is severely threatened by ongoing dolomite mining.
5. **Overgrazing and Fuelwood Collection**: Unregulated grazing and collection of firewood (though lopping branches causes less damage than clear-felling).
6. **Poaching and Illegal Trade**: Commercial hunting for tiger skins, bones, rhino horns, ivory, and medicinal herbs.
7. **Environmental Pollution & Forest Fires**: Agricultural chemical runoff, toxic industrial effluents, and uncontrolled seasonal forest fires.

#### 2. Habitat Destruction Case Study: The Himalayan Yew (*Taxus wallachiana*)
- **Nature**: A medicinal plant found in parts of Himachal Pradesh and Arunachal Pradesh.
- **Chemical Compound**: A chemical compound called **'taxol'** is extracted from its bark, needles, twigs, and roots.\n- **Medical Use**: Taxol is the biggest-selling **anti-cancer drug** in the world.\n- **Impact**: Over-exploitation has led to thousands of yew trees drying up across Himachal Pradesh and Arunachal Pradesh.\n\n---\n\n### Theme 3: Conservation of Forest and Wildlife in India\n\n#### 1. Need for Conservation\n- Preserves ecological diversity and life-support systems (water, air, and soil).\n- Preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth of species and breeding (e.g., traditional crop varieties in agriculture and fish breeding in fisheries).\n\n#### 2. Legislative Measures: The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972\n- **Enactment**: Implemented in **1972** by the Government of India.\n- **Key Features**:\n  1. An all-India list of protected species was published.\n  2. Banning hunting of specified endangered animals.\n  3. Giving legal protection to habitats and restricting trade in wildlife.\n  4. Establishment of **National Parks** and **Wildlife Sanctuaries** across central and state governments.\n  5. Special protection granted to specific threatened animals (e.g., one-horned rhinoceros, Kashmir stag / *hangul*, three types of crocodiles—freshwater, saltwater, and gharial, Asiatic lion).\n\n#### 3. Targeted Species Conservation Projects\n- **Project Tiger (1973)**:\n  - Launched in **1973** after the global tiger population dropped from 55,000 (early 20th century) to 1,827 in 1973.\n  - Threats: Poaching for bone trade/skin, habitat shrinkage, prey depletion.\n  Major Tiger Reserves in India:\n    - **Corbett National Park** (Uttarakhand)\n    - **Sunderbans National Park** (West Bengal)\n    - **Bandhavgarh National Park** (Madhya Pradesh)\n    - **Sariska Wildlife Sanctuary** (Rajasthan - Alwar)\n    - **Manas Tiger Reserve** (Assam)\n    - **Periyar Tiger Reserve** (Kerala)\n    - **Ranthambore National Park** (Rajasthan - Sawai Madhopur)\n\n---\n\n### Theme 4: Types and Distribution of Forests\n\nIn India, forest and wildlife resources are managed by the government through the **Forest Department**. Forests are classified into three administrative categories:\n\n| Forest Category | Share / Definition | Key Features / Dominant States |\n| :--- | :--- | :--- |\n| **Reserved Forests** **More than 50%** of total forest land. | Regarded as the **most valuable** for conservation of forest and wildlife resources. Local communities are strictly restricted from free access. <br>*States*: Jammu & Kashmir, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Maharashtra. |\n| **Protected Forests** **Almost one-third (1/3rd)** of total forest land. | Protected from any further depletion; limited grazing/wood collection permitted under regulation. <br>*States*: Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan. |\n| **Unclassed Forests** Other forests and wastelands belonging to government and private individuals/communities. | Managed jointly or individually by local communities and tribal bodies. <br>*States*: All North-Eastern states (e.g., Meghalaya, Nagaland) and parts of Gujarat. |\n\n*Note*: **Permanent Forest Estate** refers to Reserved and Protected forests maintained for timber and protective reasons. Madhya Pradesh has the largest area under permanent forests (75% of its total forest area).\n\n---\n\n### Theme 5: Community and Conservation\n\nTraditional local communities in India have played a vital role in forest and ecosystem conservation.\n\n#### 1. Key Community-Led Movements and Examples\n1. **Sariska Tiger Reserve (Rajasthan)**:\n   - Villagers in Sariska (Alwar, Rajasthan) fought against dolomite mining by citing the **Wildlife Protection Act**.\n2. **Bhairodev Dakav'Sonchuri'(Alwar, Rajasthan)**:\n   - Inhabitants of five villages in Alwar district declared **1,200 hectares** of forest as *Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri*.\n   - They formulated their own rules and regulations banning hunting and protecting wildlife from outside encroachments.\n3. **Chipko Movement (Uttarakhand)**:\n   - Famous environmental movement in the Himalayas where villagers (led by Sundarlal Bahuguna and local women) hugged trees to prevent logging.\n   - Successfully resisted deforestation and demonstrated that indigenous community afforestation with native species is highly effective.\n4. **Beej Bachao Andolan & Navdanya (Tehri, Uttarakhand)**:\n   - Farmers and citizen groups demonstrated that adequate levels of diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals are possible and economically viable.\n5. **Sacred Groves**:\n   - Nature worship is an age-old tribal belief that all creations of nature must be protected.\n   - **Munda and Santhal tribes** of Chota Nagpur region worship **Mahua** (*Madhuca indica*) and **Kadamba** (*Anthocephalus cadamba*) trees.\n   - **Tribals of Odisha and Bihar** worship **Tamarind** (*Tamarindus indica*) and **Mango** (*Mangifera indica*) trees during weddings.\n   - **Bishnoi Community of Rajasthan**: Blackbucks (*chinkara*), nilgai, and peacocks are protected as sacred members of the community; no one harms them.\n\n#### 2. Joint Forest Management (JFM) Program\n- **Origin**: Formal resolution passed in **1988** by the state of **Odisha**.\n- **Objective**: Involves local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests.\n- **Working Mechanism**:\n  - Local village institutions are formed to undertake protection activities on degraded forest land managed by the forest department.\n**Benefits**:\n  - In return, community members get non-timber forest produce (NTFP) and a share in the timber harvested from successful protection.\n\n---\n\n## Chronology of Historical Milestones & Policy Enactments\n\n| Year | Historical Policy / Movement Milestone | Significance / Focus |\n| :--- | :--- | :--- |\n| **1952** | Asiatic Cheetah Declared Extinct in India | Official recognition of extinct species status in India. |\n| **1952** | National Forest Policy Formulation | Target fixed at 33% geographical forest cover. |\n| **1972** | Enactment of Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act | Comprehensive legal framework for national parks & sanctuaries. |\n| **1973** | Launch of Project Tiger | Conservation initiative targeting endangered tiger population. |\n| **1973–74** | Chipko Movement in Tehri-Garhwal | Famous community tree-hugging anti-logging campaign. |\n| **1988** | First Formal Resolution of Joint Forest Management (JFM) Passed by Odisha Government to involve local village bodies. |\n\n---\n\n## Verified Previous-Year Questions (PYQs)\n\n### Question 1 (BSER Secondary Board Exam 2024 - Main Paper)\n**Question**: What is meant by'Reserved Forests'? Mention one state having a high proportion of reserved forests.
- **Mark Allocation**: 2 Marks (Short Answer)
- **Model Answer**:
  - **Definition**: Reserved forests are forest lands that are permanently earmarked for the production of timber and other forest produce, where hunting and grazing are strictly banned to ensure maximum conservation. They constitute more than 50% of India's total forest area.
  - **State Example**: Jammu & Kashmir, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Tamil Nadu, or West Bengal.

### Question 2 (BSER Secondary Board Exam 2023 - Main Paper)
**Question**: Explain any two human activities responsible for the depletion of flora and fauna in India.
- **Mark Allocation**: 2 Marks (Short Answer)
- **Model Answer**:
  1. **Expansion of Agriculture & Infrastructure**: Between 1951 and 1980, over 26,200 sq. km of forest land was converted for agricultural use. River valley projects like the Narmada Sagar project submerged over 40,000 hectares of prime forest.
  2. **Mining & Industrial Activities**: Dolomite mining in Buxa Tiger Reserve (West Bengal) has caused severe habitat destruction and blocked migration corridors of wild animals like the Asiatic elephant.

### Question 3 (BSER Secondary Board Exam 2022 - Main Paper)
**Question**: Write a short note on the Joint Forest Management (JFM) program in India.
- **Mark Allocation**: 2 Marks (Short Answer)
- **Model Answer**:
  - **Concept**: Joint Forest Management (JFM) is a cooperative program that involves local village communities in the protection and management of degraded forests alongside state forest departments.
  - **First Resolution**: Odisha was the first state to pass the official JFM resolution in **1988**.
  - **Benefits**: Local communities earn rights to non-timber forest produce (like fuelwood, fruits, and leaves) and receive a share in timber harvest profits in return for safeguarding forest tracts.

### Question 4 (BSER Secondary Board Exam 2024 - Supplementary / D&D Paper)
**Question**: Name the medicinal plant found in Himachal Pradesh and Arunachal Pradesh used to extract an anti-cancer drug.
- **Mark Allocation**: 1 Mark (Very Short Answer)
- **Model Answer**:
  - **Plant Name**: **Himalayan Yew** (*Taxus wallachiana*).
  - **Chemical Extracted**: **Taxol**.

### Practice Question 1 (Short Answer - Conceptual)
**Question**: Differentiate between Endangered species and Endemic species with suitable examples prescribed in the textbook.
- **Mark Weightage**: 2 Marks
- **Model Answer**:

| Basis | Endangered Species | Endemic Species |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Species in danger of extinction whose survival is difficult if adverse conditions persist. | Species found exclusively in specific isolated geographical areas. |
| **Geographical Distribution** | Found across various natural ranges where threatened. | Confined strictly to specific natural or island boundaries. |
| **Prescribed Examples** | Blackbuck, Crocodile, Indian wild ass, Sangai. | Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig. |

### Practice Question 2 (Short Answer - Case Study)
**Question**: Describe how traditional communities in Rajasthan actively protect forest and wildlife resources.
- **Mark Weightage**: 2 Marks
- **Model Answer**:
  1. **Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'**: Villagers of five villages in Alwar district declared 1,200 hectares of forest as *Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri*, creating their own rules against hunting and logging.
  2. **Sariska Mining Resistance**: Local villagers in Alwar fought against dolomite mining by citing provisions of the Wildlife Protection Act.
  3. **Bishnoi Community Tradition**: In Bishnoi villages of Rajasthan, herds of blackbucks (*chinkara*), nilgai, and peacocks roam freely and are protected as integral members of the community without harm.

**Question**: "Nature worship is an age-old tribal belief that has preserved several virgin forests." Justify this statement with examples of Sacred Groves from Indian tribal culture.
- **Mark Weightage**: 2 Marks
- **Official Model Answer / Marking Scheme**:
  - **Concept Explanation (1 Mark)**: Sacred Groves (*Devsthan*) are patches of virgin forests left untouched by local communities due to deep-rooted spiritual beliefs, where cutting trees or harming wildlife is strictly prohibited.
  - **Tribal Examples (1 Mark)**:
    - **Munda and Santhal tribes** of Chota Nagpur region worship **Mahua** and **Kadamba** trees during ceremonies.
    - **Tribals of Odisha and Bihar** worship **Tamarind** and **Mango** trees during wedding rituals.
