Peterson is a fascinating biographical account of Richard H. Ebright, who revolutionized molecular biology by discovering how cells read genetic code (DNA) .Growing up as an only child in Reading, Pennsylvania, Ebright had no playmates, so he channelled his energy into collecting things . By second grade, he had collected all 25 species of butterflies found around his hometown . When his interest waned, his mother gave him a children's book, The Travels of Monarch X . This book invited readers to tag monarch butterflies for research conducted by Dr. Frederick A. Urquhart of Toronto University, igniting Ebright's lifelong passion for scientific inquiry .Ebright's scientific journey progressed through a series of key milestones:County Science Fair Defeat: In seventh grade, he lost at the science fair because he presented neat slide displays of frog tissues rather than conducting real experiments. He learned that real science requires hands-on experimentation.Hormone Discovery: In high school, he investigated the 12 tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa. He proved they were not ornamental, but secreted an essential hormone required for the butterfly's full development.DNA & Cell Theory: Later at Harvard, this hormone research led Ebright to discover how cells read their genetic blueprint (DNA), a breakthrough published in the prestigious Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

### Section 1: Childhood Curiosity & Maternal EncouragementEarly Collecting Habits: Raised in Reading, Pennsylvania, Ebright spent his early childhood collecting rocks, fossils, coins, and butterflies . He was also an eager astronomer who watched stars all night.The Mother's Role: Having lost her husband when Richard was in third grade, Ebright's mother became his primary companion . She bought him telescopes, microscopes, cameras, mounting materials, and took him on educational trips . She kept him constantly engaged in learning.Section 2: The Book That Changed His Life — The Travels of Monarch XThe Turning Point: By the end of second grade, Ebright had collected all 25 local species of butterflies and was about to lose interest. His mother bought him The Travels of Monarch X by Dr. Frederick A. Urquhart .Tagging Monarchs: The book asked readers to help track monarch butterfly migration by gluing small adhesive tags to their wings and sending data to Dr. Urquhart in Canada.Rearing Butterflies at Home: Finding it hard to catch butterflies one by one, Ebright raised female monarchs in his basement, collecting eggs and watching them progress from caterpillar to pupa to adult butterfly over several years.Section 3: Learning What Real Science MeansThe Seventh-Grade Defeat: In seventh grade, Ebright entered the County Science Fair with slides of frog tissues under a microscope, but won no prizes.The Realization: Seeing winners perform actual experiments, he understood that science is not merely a neat display of facts, but an active investigation of cause and effect.Seeking Guidance: He wrote to Dr. Urquhart for project ideas, receiving a list of research suggestions that kept him busy throughout high school.Section 4: Breakthrough Research on Monarchs & ViceroysEighth-Grade Project: Ebright tested whether a viral disease killed monarch caterpillars every few years. Though his trial was inconclusive, his effort to test a real hypothesis won him a prize at the Science Fair.Viceroy Butterfly Study: He investigated why Viceroy butterflies imitate Monarch butterflies. He proved that Viceroys copy Monarchs because Monarchs taste bad to birds; by looking like Monarchs, Viceroys protect themselves from being eaten by birds. This project placed first in Zoology and third overall at the International Science Fair.Section 5: The Gold Spots, Growth Hormone & DNAThe Secret of 12 Gold Spots: In his high school senior year, Ebright investigated the 12 tiny gold spots on a monarch pupa. While many believed they were decorative, Ebright and a classmate built a device proving the spots produced a hormone necessary for the pupa to develop into an adult butterfly.Curing the Cell Code (DNA): Working with hormone tissue cultures, Ebright discovered that the hormone allows cells to divide and grow. He deduced that the chemical structure of the hormone holds the key to reading DNA—the nuclear blueprint controlling heredity and life functions.Publication & Legacy: At age 21, Ebright and his college roommate published their cell theory paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, earning international scientific acclaim.
