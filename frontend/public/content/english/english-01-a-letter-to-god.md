## 2. Chapter Metadata & Overview & OverviewMetadataChapter ID: english-01Title: A Letter to GodAuthor: G. L. FuentesGenre: Realistic Fiction / Short StorySetting: A solitary house on the crest of a low hill overlooking a valley with cornfieldsKey Characters: Lencho (hard-working farmer), Postmaster (fat, amiable postal officer), Post office employeesOverviewA Letter to God tells the story of Lencho, an innocent and hard-working farmer whose entire corn harvest is destroyed by a severe hailstorm4. Possessing an unflinching, childlike faith in God, Lencho writes a letter addressed directly to God requesting one hundred pesos to re-sow his fields and feed his family4.When the letter arrives at the local post office, the postmaster and his staff are initially amused by the address910. However, moved by Lencho's extraordinary spiritual conviction, the postmaster decides to collect money from his employees and friends as an "act of charity"10. Able to gather only seventy pesos, he mails the money signed simply as "God"11. Upon receiving seventy pesos instead of the requested hundred, Lencho displays no surprise at receiving money from God, but becomes furious at the deficit, concluding that the post office workers stole the missing thirty pesos and labeling them a "bunch of crooks"1213.

## 3. Detailed Section-Wise Explanations

### Section 1: The Promise of Harvest and "New Coins"Lencho's solitary house sits on the crest of a low hill, offering a view of the river and the ripe cornfield dotted with kidney-bean flowers4. Anticipating a good harvest, Lencho closely monitors the north-eastern sky for rain1415. When clouds gather and rain begins to fall during supper, Lencho rushes outside to experience the drops on his skin14. Overjoyed, he compares the raindrops to "new coins"—the large drops as ten-cent pieces and the smaller ones as fives—symbolizing the future income from a rich harvest1415.Section 2: The Devastating Hailstorm and DespairThe joy is brief. Strong winds begin to blow, bringing large hailstones alongside the rain7. The hailstones fall continuously for an hour, covering the entire valley in a layer of white resembling salt7. The destruction is total: not a leaf remains on the trees, the corn is ruined, and the blossoms are swept from the plants7. Standing in his ruined field, Lencho laments to his sons that even a "plague of locusts" would have left more behind, realizing his family faces a year without corn and potential hunger7.Section 3: Unshakable Faith and the First Letter to GodDespite total financial ruin, the family holds onto a single hope: help from God8. Lencho, described as "an ox of a man" due to his hard manual labor, possesses basic literacy8. Driven by the belief that God's eyes see everything, he writes a letter at daybreak on Sunday8. He explicitly asks God for 100 pesos to re-sow his land and survive until the next harvest9. He writes "To God" on the envelope, attaches a stamp, and drops it into the town mailbox9.Section 4: The Postmaster's Compassion and Act of CharityA postman discovers the unusually addressed letter and brings it to the postmaster, who breaks out laughing910. However, the postmaster soon becomes serious, deeply impressed by Lencho's profound faith10. To ensure Lencho's trust in God remains unbroken, he decides to answer the letter10. Realizing that paper and goodwill alone are insufficient, he collects money by giving part of his own salary and asking his staff and friends to contribute to an "act of charity"10. He collects seventy pesos, puts the money in an envelope addressed to Lencho, and signs it simply "God"11.Section 5: The Delivery, Reaction, and Ending IronyThe following Sunday, Lencho arrives early at the post office11. The postman hands him the envelope while the postmaster observes quietly from his office, feeling the satisfaction of a good deed11. Lencho shows no surprise upon finding money inside12. However, when he counts seventy pesos instead of a hundred, he becomes angry, convinced that God could not make a mistake nor deny his request12.He immediately writes a second letter to God, stamps it, and drops it into the mailbox1213. When the postmaster opens it, the letter reads:"God: Of the money that I asked for, only seventy pesos reached me. Send me the rest, since I need it very much. But don’t send it to me through the mail because the post office employees are a bunch of crooks. Lencho."13


## 4. Themes & Character AnalysisCore ThemesUnwavering Faith vs. Extreme Naivety: Lencho's faith in God is pure and absolute, yet so literal that he expects a postal money order from heaven4.Human Kindness and Anonymous Charity: The postmaster and postal workers demonstrate empathy and generosity by sacrificing their earnings without expecting credit1011.Irony of Human Perception: The central irony lies in Lencho accusing his anonymous benefactors—the postal staff—of being a "bunch of crooks"1316.Vulnerability of Rural Farmers: The story highlights how natural disasters can instantly destroy a farming family's livelihood7.Character AnalysisLencho:Traits: Hard-working ("an ox of a man"), deeply religious, simple-minded, innocent, distrustful of people8.Role: Primary protagonist whose absolute spiritual trust drives the entire plot48.The Postmaster:Traits: Fat, amiable, compassionate, resourceful, respectful of others' beliefs9.Role: Antagonist to Lencho's cynicism, but the moral hero who organizes financial help1011.Post Office Employees:Traits: Helpful, cooperative, generous, misunderstood1013.Role: Supporting characters who perform an act of charity, only to be ironically labeled "crooks"1013.

**Model Answer:** Lencho is a simple, hard-working farmer who lives in an isolated valley48. He is described as "an ox of a man" due to his physical labor8. His key character trait is his absolute faith in God810. When his crops are destroyed, he writes a letter directly to God asking for 100 pesos9. His innocence is evident when he shows no surprise upon receiving the money, but his lack of worldly understanding leads him to accuse the helpful postal staff of theft1213.Explain the irony at the end of the story "A Letter to God".
**Model Answer:** The ending presents a striking situational irony16. The postmaster and postal employees generously collect seventy pesos out of charity to preserve Lencho's faith in God1011. However, when Lencho finds thirty pesos missing, he believes God sent the full amount and accuses the post office staff of stealing the difference, calling them a "bunch of crooks"1213. Thus, the anonymous helpers are blamed for dishonesty.
